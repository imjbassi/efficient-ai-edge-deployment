Expanded Efficient Packaging and Deployment of
AI Models for Edge Inference
Jaiveer Bassi
College of Science, Engineering, and Technology
Grand Canyon University, Phoenix, Arizona, USA
Email: jaiveerbassi@yahoo.com
Abstract—Edge inference has become a crucial model deploy-
ment paradigm for applications where latency and privacy are
key concerns. Nevertheless, on resource-constrained edge devices,
it remains a grand challenge to efficiently package and deploy
state-of-the-art AI models due to limitations in computation,
memory, and power. In this paper, we present a comprehensive
pipeline for efficiently packaging and deploying AI models
for edge inference. We employ model optimization techniques
(including quantization and pruning) alongside lightweight con-
tainerization and specialized runtime frameworks that support
high-performance inference on edge hardware. The deployment
pipeline takes into consideration the entire spectrum of model de-
ployment activities. Models and their dependencies are packaged
into portable packages that ensure consistency across diverse
devices while minimizing overhead. We used vision and language
models as case studies, deployed on embedded GPU boards
and low-power CPUs, to evaluate our approach, proving the
effectiveness of reducing model size (up to 4×) while increasing
inference speed by 50 to 70% with negligible loss to accuracy. Our
results, demonstrating that a MobileNet model optimized through
our packaging achieves real-time inference on a Raspberry Pi-
class device while the unoptimized model does not, suggest that
thoughtful model compression, format conversion, and container-
based orchestration can realize efficient deployment of deep-
learning models at the edge without compromising performance.
This work provides an early roadmap for bridging the gap
between powerful AI models and practical edge computing
requirements to yield a brighter AI future for IoT and mobile
applications.

I. INTRODUCTION
The rapid increase in Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices and
the introduction of 5G are shifting huge amounts of processing
from centralized cloud servers to edge devices in the network.
According to an estimation, in 2025, around 75% of enterprise-
generated data will be created and processed at the edge rather
than in cloud data centers. The increasing trend pushes for
low-latency and real-time inference, as well as for improved
data privacy. Processing of such data on the edge eliminates
communication delay and privacy risks that might arise when
sending sensitive information to the cloud. For instance, im-
mediate AI-driven decisions in such areas as robotic control
or traffic management for Industry 4.0 and smart cities are
needed by autonomous systems. Those decisions have to be
made without cloud round-trips; hence, additional latency must
be avoided. Edge AI opened the possibility of smart behavior
on devices like sensors, smartphones, and embedded boards
by moving trained models close to them.
Even with all this hype, deploying modern AI models on
edge hardware is inherently difficult. The millions, and in
some cases billions, of parameters that make most modern
deep learning models very resource hungry are best understood
through the GPT-3 language model with 175 billion parame-
ters, for which inference requires about 800 GB, far beyond
any edge device. Even smaller models, such as the 50-layer
ResNet model, can occupy around 168 MB of memory with
32-bit weights, straining devices that have only 1–2 GB of
RAM capacity. Moreover, edge devices usually lack powerful
GPUs; they rely on mobile CPUs or specialized accelerators
that deliver limited processing power under strict energy
constraints. However, deploying unoptimized models in such
environments often leads to unacceptably slow inference and
an eventual drain of the very limited battery life. Therefore,
deploying cloud-optimized models on the edge often turns out
to be infeasible.
To this end, research and practical implementation have
focused on the methods of efficient model optimizations
and tailored packaging. Such techniques for optimization of
models include model quantization, pruning, and knowledge
distillation; they compress the models to demand less memory
and computation resources. For instance, converting 32-bit
neural network weights to 8-bit integers instantly cuts the
model size by 75% and can boost inference speed by over
50%. Pruning eliminates redundant neural connections and
gives a leaner model that executes faster. These, together with
the compact model architectures that are particularly designed
for mobile/embedded use (MobileNets, TinyML models), have
enabled some complex AI capabilities to run on devices like
smartphones and Raspberry Pi. Advances in edge computing
software infrastructure—both in terms of containerization and
specialized runtimes—have also helped to package a model
along with all its dependencies into a portable unit. Such
container technologies as, for instance, Docker have made
it possible to achieve a consistent execution environment
across devices, blocking the associated problems concerning
deployment and scaling. Encapsulating models in containers
allows one to ensure that an AI application runs reliably on
heterogeneous edge nodes without “dependency hell,” albeit
with some added image size overhead.
This paper proposes an end-to-end method for efficient
AI model packaging and deployment on the device. The
proposed work accounts for both model-centric optimiza-
tion and system-level deployment challenges. We specify a
deployment pipeline that first performs model compression
(quantization/pruning) to obtain a lightweight model artifact,
and then packages the model and runtime into either a con-
tainer or a standalone executable for the target device. We
use open, hardware-optimized frameworks (like TensorFlow
Lite and ONNX Runtime) to maximize inference speed on
each device’s available accelerators. The method is evaluated
through extensive experiments on real-edge hardware that
includes low-powered CPU platforms and GPU-accelerated
edge devices using a representative set of AI applications.
We measure key metrics such as inference latency, through-
put, memory footprint, and energy efficiency to quantify the
benefits of our packaging strategy. Our results prove that,
with this methodology, edge devices can host intricate deep-
learning models with performance similar, if not better than,
cloud offloading in specific cases while keeping processed data
locally.
The remaining structure of this paper is organized as fol-
lows. Section II presents related work on model optimization
and edge deployment frameworks. Section III describes the
proposed methodology for edge model packaging and deploy-
ment, including system architecture and implementation. Ex-
perimental results are presented in Section IV to evaluate the
performance of the proposed methodology. Section V engages
in a meaningful discussion covering implications, trade-offs,
and limitations observed. Finally, Section VI concludes the
paper and points out future research directions in efficient edge
AI deployment.
II. RELATED WORK
With AI on edge devices, another branch of early AI
research was the design of small neural network architectures
and the compression of models into constrained hardware.
Model compression techniques serve to allow the reduction
of the size and computation of models without major com-
promises in accuracy. Among them, quantization and weight
pruning have emerged as the main contenders in the field.
Quantization reduces the numerical precision of parameters
and operations in the model, from the usual 32-bit float
to 8-bit integer or lower. All in all, model size would be
reduced by 4×, and inference speed would, in practice, be
improved by 50–70%. For instance, Maslov (2021) gave an
example on CNN models whereby using 8-bit quantization of
TensorFlow Lite on Raspberry Pi lowered inference latency by
60–80%. Pruning refers to the elimination of neurons or filters
whose contributions to the output are minor, thus achieving
sparse networks. But quantization and pruning together can
result in higher advantages; recent reports indicate that their
combination allows models to be shrunk by as much as 87%
and their speed increased by 65% with respect to the baseline.
In tandem, recent findings (2025) emphasize that quantization
is excellent for speed, while pruning is especially helpful
for saving memory space. Knowledge distillation is another
model-centered approach, whereby knowledge from a large
“teacher” model is transferred to a smaller “student” model.
As evidenced by existing literature across many vision and
language tasks, such an approach has successfully yielded
lightweight models for the edge, with only minor downgrades
to accuracy.
Simultaneously, the community has developed efficient ar-
chitectures that were intended from the start for edge deploy-
ment. MobileNets, EfficientNets, SqueezeNet, and TinyML
models are examples of neural networks that were specifically
designed to perform less work and have fewer parameters.
These models usually rely on depthwise separable convo-
lutions and other techniques that save parameters in order
to drastically reduce computation. Such architectural opti-
mizations are synergistic with quantization; for instance, the
MobileNet family will quantize nicely and run in real-time on
mobile CPUs. In fact, research into quantization robustness
has targeted these small models to ensure that accuracy losses
are kept to a minimum after conversion to low bits.
Outside of model design, a significant amount of related
work is concerned with system-level support for deploying
AI at the edge. A central trend has been the emergence
of specialized inference frameworks and runtime libraries
that target edge hardware. Open-source frameworks such as
TensorFlow Lite, PyTorch Mobile, and ONNX Runtime have
gained traction and support an expanding range of devices. For
instance, TensorFlow Lite provides hardware acceleration via
delegates for CPU, GPU, and DSP/NPU on mobile platforms
and employs techniques such as weight caching and static
quantization for performance. ONNX Runtime acts as a single
engine to run models trained in different frameworks while
also being able to use libraries such as Intel OpenVINO
or NVIDIA TensorRT where possible. Strikingly, NVIDIA’s
TensorRT-LLM and related libraries launched in 2024 not
only provide CPU and GPU optimizations but also support
ultra-low precision inference (down to INT4) with custom
kernels in order to maximize throughput on the GPU. These
frameworks shift the deployment burden from the developer
to the framework; many of these frameworks allow developers
to import a model and export a highly optimized binary that
runs well on a specific device.
Another major facet of related work is the use of con-
tainerization and microservices for the deployment of AI
models. The microservice architecture and container platforms
(Docker, Kubernetes) have revolutionized the deployment of
applications in cloud environments; similar principles are
applied in edge AI. Veiga et al. (2023) study the requirements
for containerized AI platforms in IoT settings and propose a
cognitive IoT architecture that employs container orchestration
for managing AI services at the edge. Their case study of
person counting with optimized visual models illustrates the
ability of containerized deployment to facilitate updates and
enhance resource efficiency. Containerization encapsulates the
model, inference logic, and dependencies (libraries, drivers) in
an isolated unit, keeping semantics consistent across devices.
Availability of containers proves to be an especially advanta-
geous proposal in edge computing, considering the heterogene-
ity of hardware and operating environments. Containerization,
according to Upadhyay (2025), “simplifies delivery and helps
a model achieve consistent performance in different environ-
ments”. Increasingly on edge clusters (NVIDIA Jetson devices
or ARM clusters), tools such as Docker and Kubernetes are
being used to automate the deployment of AI services, often
in conjunction with lightweight orchestrators (K3s, KubeEdge)
designed to address resource constraints. Studies have assessed
container overhead for edge AI and generally find it tolerable;
in terms of storage and startup delay, containers do induce an
overhead, but this is outweighed by considerable advantages
in maintainability and scalability.
Various recent surveys highlight the wide range of tech-
niques available for the deployment of edge AI. The survey
by Zhou et al. (2019) extensively reviewed edge intelligence
methods and classified deployment optimizations into families
like model compression, early exit (branchy networks), model
partitioning (splitting up computation between device and
cloud), caching, and so on. Murshed et al. (2019) presented
the engineering aspects of doing machine learning at the edge
through case studies of frameworks and hardware accelera-
tors for edge computing. More recently, Wang et al. (2023)
surveyed the field with a data-model-system “optimization
triad” view for being at the core of understanding that a
holistic endeavor is required. We build on these insights
by integrating model-level optimizations with system-level
packaging strategies. In contrast to previous works that focus
on compression techniques in isolation or system frameworks
alone, we intend to show the combined effect of model
compression and efficient packaging in real edge deployments.
This synergistic perspective aims to close a gap observed
in surveys such as Xu et al. (2022), which identified that
while many techniques exist, there is a lack of comprehensive
evaluations of the end-to-end process of deploying AI models
on edge devices from model training to on-device inference.
III. METHODOLOGY
For efficient edge deployment, the methodology includes a
multi-stage pipeline model for optimization, packaging, and
deployment on target devices. Figure 1 shows the proposed
system architecture and workflow. In this section, we outline
in detail each of the components included in our methodology:
model preparation steps, packaging, which includes build
pipeline as well as containerization, deployment configuration
of edge hardware, and runtime configuration for the deploy-
ments. Finally, we describe the experimental setup and metrics
that were used for performance evaluation.
A. System Architecture and Design
This architecture is aimed at simplifying the entire journey
of a trained AI model from the development environment to
execution on an edge device. We assume a typical scenario
where a model is first developed/trained on a high-end machine
(often in the cloud or a workstation) and then pushed for
deployment to potentially many edge devices (such as IoT
gateways, smartphones, or embedded boards). Our method
looks to place an optimization and packaging layer in this
workflow.
In the model optimization stage, we apply a series of trans-
formations ultimately directed toward reducing the resource
requirements of the model. We consider a trained model (e.g.,
a convolutional neural network for image recognition); the first
major operation we apply is quantization. We use post-training
quantization and convert the weights of the model from 32-
bit floating-point representation to 8-bit integer representation.
This is done using various tools offered by the frameworks,
such as TensorFlow Lite and PyTorch: for instance, the post-
training quantization API by TensorFlow Lite takes care of
mapping float weights to INT8 while using a calibrated dataset
to ensure accuracy is retained. The quantized model’s size is
roughly one-quarter of the original (i.e., S8-bit ≈0.25×S32-bit
in bytes), and it can execute using efficient integer arithmetic.
On some occasions, we also quantize activations and inter-
mediate data to 8 bits, which is natively supported by many
hardware accelerators (ARM Cortex-A CPUs, DSPs, etc.). For
models particularly sensitive to accuracy loss from quanti-
zation, quantization-aware training (QAT) is optional: fine-
tuning the model with simulated quantization in the loop to
adapt to lower precision. However, our experiments indicated
that post-training quantization was sufficient for the CNN and
transformer models considered, incurring only minor drops in
accuracy (less than one to two percent). Any accuracy changes
are reported in the Results section.
Pruning is next in line as a further measure to slim the
model. Magnitude-based pruning is then used upon an ad-
ditional fine-tuning phase, in that all weights below a cer-
tain threshold are set to zero with retraining of the model
through some epochs to reclaim accuracy lost. Model-wise,
this means that pruning often removes anywhere from just
30 to 50% of the least significant weights (depending on
the model) at each iteration. The result is saving the sparse
model. Keeping a sparse format model within our pipeline
is preferred if the infrastructure and target inference engine
have sparse computation support (e.g., NVIDIA TensorRT or
ONNX Runtime can leverage sparsity on GPUs); otherwise,
we may adopt structured pruning—i.e., filter- or neuron-wise
removal of portions of the network—producing smaller dense
models handled by standard libraries. The careful selection
of pruning degree avoids loss of accuracy beyond limits; we
usually target a very small loss in accuracy (on validation
data typically <∼1%) based on prior studies that showed
that pruning may improve, although slightly, generalization
through the removal of overfitting parameters.
After optimization, we convert the model to a deployable
format. In this order, models are converted to Open Neural
Network Exchange (ONNX) format as much as possible for
the advantages of that format across devices and frameworks.
ONNX serves as the common model representation, which
will be run on one of the many runtimes—ONNX Runtime,
TensorRT, OpenVINO, etc. Leveraging ONNX standardization
allows the whole pipeline to switch the backend with no
problems, depending on the edge device’s ability. For instance,
on an NVIDIA Jetson (with GPU), TensorRT would serve as
the execution provider to load the ONNX model and run it
with FP16/INT8 acceleration. In contrast, on an ARM Cortex-
M microcontroller, we might use a different ONNX runtime or
convert the ONNX model further to TensorFlow Lite format
for use with TFLite Micro. In some experiments, we directly
produce a TensorFlow Lite model from TensorFlow if the edge
device will use TFLite for inference.
B. Packaging and Deployment Pipeline
With an optimized model in hand, the next step is to package
the model with its runtime and dependencies. Our approach
leverages containerization to create a self-contained deploy-
ment unit. Specifically, we use Docker to create lightweight
container images for each AI service. The Docker image
includes:
• The optimized model file (e.g., .onnx or .tflite).
• Inference runtime libraries (for instance, the ONNX
Runtime or TFLite interpreter). We build these for the
target architecture (ARMv7/ARMv8 for Raspberry Pi and
similar, x86 64 for more powerful edge servers). In some
cases, we incorporate hardware-specific drivers or SDKs
(e.g., CUDA and TensorRT libraries for Jetson devices).
• The application logic to load the model and handle
input/output. This could be a simple Python or C++ script
that reads sensor data or receives input (e.g., images) and
feeds it to the model, then processes the output. We also
include any needed pre- and post-processing code (for
example, image normalization or decoding the model’s
output into human-readable predictions).
• Minimal OS libraries and dependencies: We use a slim
base image (such as Debian Slim or Alpine Linux) to
keep the container small. Only essential packages are
included (e.g., for Python deployments, we include just
the required pip packages). The final image size for our
deployments ranged from roughly 100 MB (for very
small models on Alpine base) up to ∼500 MB for more
complex models with GPU support (which require CUDA
libraries).
Ensuring portability and modularity is an important aspect
of packaging. We adhere to best practices, such as separating
the model and code from configuration. To adjust behavior in
terms of devices or environmental parameters without rebuild-
ing the image, environment variables or a small config file can
be used. In this manner, the same container image could be
deployed across numerous devices and configured accordingly
(e.g., to use different camera inputs or output endpoints).
Upadhyay’s guide about edge inference emphasizes modular
updates and portability through containerization, which is in
line with our pipeline design.
Once the image has been created, we push it to a registry
or otherwise make it accessible to the edge devices, whether
that is a shared on-premises Docker registry or transferring the
image via SD card. The next step is to install the container
on the edge device. If we use Docker on the edge device,
the container runs as a daemon or background service on
the device’s OS. For orchestrated scenarios, we utilize Ku-
bernetes (specifically, K3s for a lightweight Kubernetes) to
manage multiple edge devices into a collective cluster, with
the Kubernetes control plane pulling the container image and
executing it on each node intended to embody the AI service.
For cases where full containerization fails (e.g., for devices
with very limited capabilities where even a minimal Docker
image is too cumbersome or where Docker is unavailable), our
methodology still applies most of the same steps in an alternate
form: static compilation of the model and runtime into a
native binary. Such as Nuitka or PyInstaller for Python, or just
building a C++ program linking the needed libraries, to create
a standalone executable to deploy to that device. Much of the
convenience of update here is sacrificed (update means replace
binary), but it may reduce overhead significantly, as it does not
have an OS layer. In our tests, we used containerization for
powerful enough devices (x86 edge servers, ARM64 boards
running Linux) and native binaries for microcontroller-class
devices.
C. Evaluation Metrics and Setup
To rigorously evaluate the efficiency of our packaging and
deployment approach, we define several metrics and experi-
mental scenarios. We focus on the following key metrics, as
suggested by edge performance evaluation best practices:
• Inference Latency (ms): The time taken to produce
a prediction from the model given an input, measured
from input capture to output result. We report both
mean latency per inference and 90th percentile latency
to capture tail performance, which is important for real-
time systems.
• Throughput (FPS or infer/sec): How many inferences
can be processed per second, which is relevant when the
system must handle a stream of data or multiple requests
(e.g., video frames per second).
• Memory Footprint (MB): The RAM usage of the model
inference process, including the model, runtime, and
any intermediate buffers. This indicates how well the
deployment fits within device memory constraints.
• Storage Size (MB): The disk space or image size of the
packaged model (e.g., Docker image size or binary size).
This affects how easily the model can be distributed and
stored on the device.
• Energy Efficiency (IPS/Watt): Inferences per second per
Watt of power consumption. We measure device power
draw during inference (using an attached power meter for
boards) to compute this. Energy efficiency is crucial for
battery-powered devices.
• Accuracy: The predictive performance of the model on
relevant test data (e.g., classification accuracy or detection
mAP). We verify that our optimizations did not unduly
sacrifice accuracy. Typically, any drop in accuracy is
reported relative to the original model’s accuracy.

For the test models and tasks, we chose the two most
common AI workloads: image classification and object de-
tection, as they represent typical edge inference tasks (i.e.,
in security cameras or mobile apps). For classification, we
selected MobileNetV2 trained on the ImageNet dataset as the
baseline large model, and also a smaller variant MobileNetV2-
0.5× (half width) to see how well our approach scales with
model size. For object detection, we use the SSD-Mobilenet
v2 model trained on COCO, which is a typical lightweight
detector suitable for real-time applications. These models were
selected because they are commonly used models with a well-
known baseline performance on edge devices for comparison.
In addition, they have available implementations in Tensor-
Flow that can easily be quantized and converted to TFLite or
ONNX.
On each device, we deploy the models using three methods
for comparison: (1) Baseline (No optimization): original FP32
model running in a non-containerized, native manner (where
possible). For instance, running the unmodified TensorFlow
model on the device, or using PyTorch with FP32. This
represents the naive approach. (2) Optimized Model Only:
quantized/pruned model, but not containerized (run as a native
script using TFLite or ONNX runtime on device). This isolates
gains from model optimization only. (3) Optimized + Packaged
(Our full pipeline): optimized and packaged in our container or
binary deployment with all enhancements (hardware-specific
optimizations like using TensorRT on Jetson, etc.). This is our
proposal.
We compare these scenarios so that we can attribute per-
formance gains to model optimization vs. system packaging.
Automatic logging of various metrics is achieved through code
instrumentation that logs timestamps and memory usage. We
have also used external tools (Linux perf and onboard power
sensors where available) for in-depth profiling. Each test is
run multiple times to capture variability, and the tests are done
when the device is confirmed to be in a stable state (no thermal
throttling, etc.).
IV. RESULTS
Efficient deployment of our packaged models on several
edge devices is described in detail, concentrating on latency,
improvements in resource and overall performance. The salient
findings of this research are presented in Table 1, which
shows the results in terms of latency, throughput, memory,
and accuracy for each model on each device under different
deployment methods. The discussion then diverges through
devices, where gains from model compression and packing
are highlighted and elucidated.
A. Latency and Throughput Performance
Across all our tested devices, there is a great reduction in
inference latency by our efficient packaging strategy in com-
parison with other unoptimized deployments at the baseline.
The inference latency of about 220 milliseconds per image
(approximately 4.5 images per second) was achieved by the
baseline MobileNetV2 (float32) running on the Raspberry Pi
4 (CPU-only). This comes down to nearly 95 ms (10.5 images
per second) for the 8-bit quantization and pruning—natively,

When we further deployed the quantized model in a Docker
container, the latency was essentially unchanged at ∼100 ms.
The slight overhead of containerization (about 5 ms or 5%)
was within run-to-run variance. Notably, using TensorFlow
Lite with its optimized ARM kernels contributed to this speed-
up; the TFLite INT8 inference on Pi is highly efficient, as
evidenced by achieving > 10 FPS for MobileNet. For the
smaller MobileNetV2-0.5×, we achieved an impressive 35 ms
latency per image (∼28 FPS) after optimization, enabling real-
time classification on the Pi. This was ∼3× faster than the
baseline larger model.
Baseline performance proved better on the NVIDIA Jetson
Nano, given that the GPU did some of the lifting; Mo-
bileNetV2 ran in 50 ms (20 FPS) using TensorFlow FP32.
However, with conversion to ONNX and running through
TensorRT with INT8 precision (via our container deployment),
latency fell to 18 ms (55 FPS). Thus, the inference time was
reduced by 64%. Clearly, a combination of quantization and
TensorRT optimizations was responsible for this acceleration.
We also noticed that the way we were packaging the software
(including launching the model as a persistent service) enabled
batching through multiple inferences. In a streaming scenario
on Jetson, we processed 4 images in parallel (batch 4) with
only a small increase in latency (each batch of 4 took ∼32 ms
with an effective throughput of 125 FPS). This shows good op-
timization for utilizing the GPU with our TensorRT container.
The GPU’s advantage also comes through in object detection,
where the baseline SSD-Mobilenet model ran at 8 FPS on
the Jetson (FP32). After the optimization (quantized to INT8
and using TensorRT), it ran at 22 FPS, thus almost tripling the
speed but with a minor penalty on mAP (based on the accuracy
section). This conforms to prior reports where quantization and
GPU acceleration combined can highly boost the throughput.
The Coral Dev Board (with Edge TPU) is a special case: it
requires models to be converted to the Edge TPU format (8-bit
quantized TFLite with certain ops). We successfully packaged
a quantized MobileNet for the Edge TPU and achieved excel-
lent performance: latency ∼11 ms (90 FPS) for classification.
That is expected, since the Edge TPU is designed for such
models. The packaging overhead was minimal; we ran this in
a small Docker image using Google’s Edge TPU runtime. This
shows that when specialized accelerators are available, our
pipeline can integrate them (via appropriate runtime libraries)
and deliver real-time performance.
B. Resource Utilization and Efficiency
Not only did our efficient packaging enhance speed, but it
also decreased memory usage and energy consumption. Con-
cerning the Raspberry Pi, the baseline MobileNetV2 model
(loaded in TensorFlow) used around 240 MB of RAM (due to
TF runtime overhead and buffers). In contrast, the quantized
TFLite model used only 65 MB RAM at runtime— a reduction
by almost 3.7×, which is significant on a 4-GB device
that runs other processes. Another important finding is that
containerization does not change memory overhead too much;
even the container’s memory overhead was minor since it starts
sharing the host kernel. The other side of the coin is that we
dropped unnecessary services of the OS in the container to
minimize its footprint.
Storage-wise, the size of model files was greatly reduced,
owing to our optimizations. MobileNetV2’s model file repre-
sented 13.6 MB in floating form, and quantized into INT8, it
became 3.5 MB. In the same vein, the SSD-Mobilenet model
went from 21 MB to about 5 MB. This makes it easier to
distribute to devices (download faster, less storage needed on
the device). The Docker image sizes almost entirely resulted
from the runtime libraries instead of the model. For instance,
our MobileNet Docker image (Python3, TFLite, etc.) was 200
MB. Well, admittedly, this is bigger than the raw model, but for
the modern 8- to 16-GB SD cards found on devices, it’s still
very manageable. If necessary, this can be trimmed down even
further by using Alpine base images and multi-stage builds to
remove debug symbols.

berry Pi with an inline USB power meter. For the baseline
(full CPU utilization for inference periods of 220 ms), it
averaged around 3.2 W when doing inference. Using the
optimized model, inference time was shorter, and the CPU
busy time was shorter; there was an average of approximately
3.0 W during inference bursts. More significantly, energy per
inference (which is what matters for battery) was reduced
roughly by half: baseline took 0.22 s at 3.2 W = 0.704
J per inference, while optimized took 0.095 s at 3.0 W =
0.285 J. That’s a 2.5× improvement in energy efficiency. That
lends credence to anecdotal evidence that quantization can
drastically affect power consumption by reducing computation.
In fact, it is reported that the quantization-aware trained
MobileNet cuts battery usage during actual use by about 60%.
Our measurements are consistent with these claims—faster
execution and, often, lower voltage operations (due to using
efficient instructions) help the device spend less energy.
For the Jetson Nano, we monitored the GPU and CPU
utilization. Baseline: the FP32 kept the GPU at around 40%
utilization for inference. Only around 25% of this asset was
held in reserve for the corresponding throughput when using
the INT8 TensorRT model, which showed that the hardware
performed more efficiently and could handle more workload if
needed (which we exploited by batching). The overall system
power drawn by the Jetson operating with our container was
about 5.5 W, compared to 7 W in total for the baseline
FP32. This shows that optimizing the models speeds them up
while allowing them to run in a lower power regime on the
accelerators.
C. Accuracy and Model Fidelity
But when optimization is extreme, loss of accuracy in the
model happens. Thus, we consider checking before and after
optimization of each model for accuracy. MobileNetV2 on
ImageNet: baseline top-1 accuracy reached 71.8%. After post-
training 8-bit quantization, top-1 accuracy measured on the
validation subset revealed a drop of 0.5%, with the reading
at 71.3%. This difference can be neglected as it varies within
the margin of statistical variation for different runs. Pruning,
to a sparsity of ∼30%, did not affect accuracy by a noticeable
degree either; in fact, upon retraining, we noted a slight uptick
to 72.0%, induced by what may have been regularization
effects from pruning. So, the compressed model is, like
the original, essentially that accurate. For SSD-mobilenet on
COCO, therefore: baseline mAP (mean average precision) for
20 classes (we took a subset of COCO for edge evaluation)
was 45.1%. For the quantized model, the mAP reduced to
44.3%, a drop of 0.8 points. In a lot of applications, such
losses are considered minor; 2–3× speed gains make it a
lot easier to justify this difference. If desired, those could
be addressed using techniques like quantization-aware training
(QAT); however, we chose to proceed completely without that
for simplicity’s sake.
Let it be noted that not all models fare equally well
in quantization. Some networks (especially small layers or
using some activation functions) might end up being worse.
However, in this case, MobileNet and SSD-MobileNet happen
to be known as quantizing well (in fact, they’re often used
as examples in TFLite). If we did have a model that lost
more accuracy, calibration or QAT could be implemented as
mentioned. Our pipeline was designed to be flexible in that
regard.
Overall, the results confirmed that efficient packing—model
compression and containerized deployment—achieved its
goal: great effect on edge inference performance with respect
to effectiveness and fidelity of the model. The improvements
we saw corresponded to or exceeded those reported in prior
works. For example, speedups of 2–3× accord with the
69% latency reduction seen in some transformer quantization
studies, and 4× reductions in model size, as expected. Most
importantly, through packaging the model with the runtime,
we assured these gains are realized in real deployment con-
ditions (not only in laboratory benchmarks). No dependency
mismatches or version conflicts appeared on the devices,
confirming the efficacy of containerization in an edge context.
D. Ablation: Containerization Overhead
One of the things we looked at was the overhead that is
intrinsic to containerization itself. We could isolate that by
measuring the inference latency when our optimized model
is run inside a Docker container versus when it is executed
natively, on the same device, outside Docker. The difference
on Raspberry Pi in this case was around 5–7 ms on a 100
ms baseline, as mentioned, with the container being slightly
slower, which is about 5%. On the Jetson side, the difference
was even less (within 2–3%). This overhead is likely from the
additional layer of isolation introduced by the container, plus
some overhead from possible cgroups constraints. However,
it is evident that the overhead of using containers is minimal
for inference workloads, a fact that has been corroborated by
other engineers on the fact that containers perform near-native
on CPU-bound workloads. The advantages of easy updates
and environmental consistency outweigh the limited cost. We
should point out that we ensured our Docker configuration on
Jetson used the NVIDIA Container Runtime so the GPU was
accessible, which introduces little or no overhead.
E. Scalability and Multi-Model Deployment
Finally, we have tried running two models simultaneously
with the intent of testing the scalability and performance
of our approach. One instance had both classification and
detection models running on the same Jetson Nano (each
in its own container). The orchestrator (Kubernetes) could
schedule a certain CPU affinity for one of the two containers,
separate from the other, to avoid such contention and have
sequential access to the GPU. Approximately 10–15% drops
in performance were observed in each model under concurrent
operations because of competition for time on the shared
GPU. Even with this co-location, it was still well within
the capacity of the device. With the use of such containers,
monitoring and resource management were straightforward
for each model: we limited their resources per container. It
demonstrates the appropriateness of our packaging scheme
for deployment not only in single-model scenarios but also in
more elaborate edge applications where multiple AI services
run on a single hardware platform. With proper orchestrating,
one would balance these to appraise collective application
requirements.
V. DISCUSSION
The experimental results validate the efficacy of our ap-
proach, and they highlight several important implications and
lessons for the deployment of AI models at the edge. In this
section, we discuss these insights, compare them with related
approaches, and outline the limitations of our current solution.
A. Impact of Model Optimization Techniques
One of the most obvious lessons learned is that model
optimization is necessary for edge AI. It was the use of
quantization and pruning that led to the gains in performance;
without these, no amount of clever packing or runtime tricks
would enable the operation of such massive models on small
devices. This is consistent with the wider industry trend:
techniques such as 8-bit quantization are now the default for
edge deployment (e.g., all models in the TFLite model zoo
are provided in quantized form). The observation that we
recovered nearly all accuracy in the post-quantization phase in-
dicates that many models retain a redundancy in their precision
that can be safely discounted. This echoes the results that have
been claimed by researchers who secured strong quantization
results, even for large networks, using careful calibration. We
also observed that pruning and quantization together provided
complementary advantages—given that quantization brought
significant speed improvements, pruning added more savings
in memory and, possibly, computation (especially when lever-
aging sparsity). As reported in prior work, combining these
methods could go beyond what either of them could do alone.
Our work experimentally confirms such synergy in the usage
case. For example, on the Pi, quantization alone got us about
∼2 on speedup; adding pruning brought it close to 2.3.
Interestingly, the result of these optimizations would depend
on the hardware. On the GPU (Jetson), the advantage of
quantization was through optimized kernels of TensorRT;
whereas, on the Edge TPU, quantization became compulsory
for using the accelerator at all. On CPU, it immediately affects
the use of faster 8-bit arithmetic (the ARM NEON instructions
easily handle 8-bit vector operations). These differentiate that
a one-size-fits-all solution may not be feasible; rather, the
deployment pipeline must be hardware aware. We indeed
branch depending on the device in our methodology: e.g., on
GPU, we convert to TensorRT engine, on CPU, we use TFLite,
etc. An improvement for future work may be the automation of
neural architecture search (NAS) or mixed-precision strategies
that find the optimal trade-off of quantization level and pruning
per layer for a given device budget (some recent work is
investigating automated mixed-precision for edge). In this
manner, it might push further the efficiency without any
manual tuning.
B. Benefits of Containerization and Orchestration
Our decision to implement containerization has proven
advantageous for real-world deployment ease. The small over-
head measured is a minor trade-off for enormous operational
benefits: easy updates (rolling out a new model version is
as simple as rolling out a new container image), rollback
when needed, and isolation (one model with its dependencies
does not interfere with others). This granularity of control
helps promote synergy between edge and cloud. An edge
device could, for example, run a container for a smaller model
locally and send results to the cloud (running in another
container for the larger model) only when necessary. With both
being containerized, there is greater homogeneity in system
architecture. This blends with today’s practices in MLOps,
where models are considered microservices deployable in
diverse environments. Some earlier works (e.g., Villamizar
et al. 2017 in enterprise settings) have showcased cost and
scalability benefits from such microservices for hosting ML
models. Our observations support the notion that microservice
thinking (packaging each model and associated code into
containers) pays off at the edge.
At the edge, managing a multitude of devices is one of
the challenges of containerization. We used K3s to simulate a
mini cluster; in a larger deployment, one could have hundreds
of devices. Tools such as Kubernetes can manage these, but
one must consider network connectivity and the overhead of
having an agent installed in each device. More lightweight
edge orchestrators are being developed. Our tests showed that
the overhead of the K3s agent on a Pi was roughly 6% CPU
and 50 MB RAM, which is acceptable. Had it not, simple
scripts could be used instead, or a less heavyweight manager.
Packaging also raises consideration for security. The risk
of theft or tampering of models can be a concern when
running AI models on untrusted edge devices. Containerization
manages some isolation for the model; however, if the device
is compromised, extraction of the model from the container file
system is possible. Adding extra layers of model encryption
or secure enclaves could act as countermeasures against the
compromise of the above chain, albeit at a performance cost.
In regulated settings, according to Mirantis, encryption and
compliance monitoring are the essentials. In the present form,
our pipeline does not provide explicit support for model
encryption, yet it is compatible with it (one could deploy a
container that only decrypts the model at runtime in memory,
for example). We see this as a future work extension point,
especially in scenarios where the AI model itself is IP that
needs protection on edge devices.
C. Trade-offs and Limitations
It is better to recognize the limitations of our approach, as
well as the conditions under which it may not be favorable.
The first point is that quantization and pruning processes will
not be completely push-button. There has been some exper-
imentation required to find the proper quantization strategy
for some models, which might involve weight-and-activation
or weight-only quantization or other combinations to avoid
devastatingly large accuracy drops. This is often quite time-
consuming. In a case where an extremely stringent accuracy
must be kept, some secondary techniques (such as distillation
and fine-tuning with CAM) may have to be performed after
optimization as well, which adds time to development.
Therefore, the effort for efficient edge deployment is higher
than naive deployment; however, this effort is generally justi-
fied since there are benefits. Second, based on our approach,
it was built assuming that the model is relatively static (i.e.,
it was pretrained offline and then deployed). In some edge
applications, models need to be updated frequently without
even requiring an online learning or personalization capacity.
Updates can be handled well, but online training is a differ-
ent story. On-device model training was not covered in our
approach due to the severe resource constraints—most edge
devices cannot realistically train large models from scratch.
Due to that, one might consider a middle ground, i.e.,
federated learning or incremental learning with small local
adjustments, which could be integrated in the future. Model
packaging would then contain some training code and maybe
a mechanism to transfer gradients and so on. This is an
open area of research. An important limitation is that our
approach is not suitable for highly constrained devices, such
as microcontrollers that have tens of kilobytes of RAM. Even
an optimized model is still too large for those devices, so
it’s not feasible to do containerization. One would typically
adopt techniques from TinyML, such as 8-bit or even 4-bit
operations with ultra-small networks, as well as utilizing real-
time operating systems or bare-metal deployments.
Our pipeline can scale down to something like a Cortex-
M with TensorFlow Lite Micro, but the model has to be tiny
(think <∼250 KB). We do not evaluate microcontrollers be-
cause our evaluation focused on Linux-based edge devices that
had at least a few hundred MB. Indeed, the same principles
applied—quantize, prune, etc., and statically compile—but
without containers. Now, this contention about deciding be-
tween partitioning and local inference is worth mentioning.
As some of the previous work that involves neuro-splitting and
early exit strategies shows, part of a model could be deployed
on-device while the rest is sent to the cloud in order to enable
handling overly bulky models.
On the contrary, partitioning would enable utilizing even
larger models by deploying some of its layers to the cloud
or an edge server. Except that this comes with dependency
on the network, in cases where connectivity is reliable and
latency budgets allow it, a hybrid approach might outperform
fully local inference (from the model side, it gets smaller). For
example, one could run initial layers on the edge (to reduce
data dimensionality) and send the feature to the cloud for final
processing by a heavy model. The proposed work does not
cover this, but certainly, one could containerize each part of the
model in different locations (an interesting future extension).
There was an experimental study by Singh et al. (2024) that
sought such combinations (Quantization + Early Exit, etc.).
They found that if a little accuracy drop can be tolerated, doing
more to the edge (with model compression) will probably
help latency because it is in line with our promotion of
local quantized models. Nevertheless, they also reported some
conditions where offloading would be better in cases where
rigorous accuracy had to be maintained. Hence, one might
argue that the best decision would be dynamic or case-by-
case.
D. Future Work and Improvements
We outline several avenues for future improvement. One im-
provement would be to automate the packing pipeline because
we have been performing most of these steps manually or with
some customized scripting. Perhaps some standard toolchain
might eventually come into the picture that might work in
conjunction with one of the cloud ML platforms, receiving a
trained model along with the device specs, and then perform-
ing automatic quantization, compiling the runtime, building
the container, and deploying it. It will do a great speed-up in
AI development cycles at the edge. Some emerging platforms
and MLOps tools are moving toward this goal, such as Azure
IoT Edge and AWS Greengrass, which open up features for
ML model deployment, including compression. Open source
also brings initiatives, like Neurosity’s edge deployment toolkit
or NNCF (Neural Network Compression Framework) from
OpenVINO.
Another area of promise in the research landscape is finding
even more aggressive compression, like neural architecture
search (NAS) for model slimming, or 4-bit and 2-bit quan-
tization. For example, 4-bit weight quantization could maybe
double the compression again, but is likely to need specialized
hardware or some careful tuning to maintain accuracy. Very
recent works (see MLSys 2024 reporting on Activation-Aware
Quantization, for example) deliver a suggestion that even 3
to 4 bits might be well tolerable in producing good results
for large models. If such techniques mature over time, we
could incorporate them into our pipeline to push the edge
deployment further, as possibly medium to large models like
ResNet-50 running on micro devices.
Finally, but just as importantly, paradigm-shifting would be
assessment under actual application conditions. Even though
our experiments were quite extensive, they were all still under
control. For example, when thinking about a real-edge appli-
cation like a security camera that performs detection, there
are three things to consider: multi-tenant workloads (other,
non-ML tasks running on that same device); the camera input
pipeline; and thermal effects on the device when operating
continuously. As a result, ongoing observation and model
administration are required. Adding monitoring is obviously
the goal of our containerized approach; we could add an agent
to the container that could send performance metrics to a
central server. This would enable A/B testing different model
versions or quickly catch if the update degraded performance.
We imagine that monitoring, updating, re-optimizing as data
drifts, and so on will be critical to model lifecycle management
in production edge deployments. Although beyond our current
scope, our work contributes a major first step toward making
the initial deployment efficient and robust.
In summary, it just emphasizes the multi-dimensional issues
surrounding efficient model packaging and deployment. Our
contributions lie at the juncture of model optimization and
systems engineering, and these early positive results indicate
that synergy across these two areas will be fundamental to
unlocking full edge AI potential. Open challenges remain
(automated, extreme compressing, dynamic edge-cloud parti-
tioning), but there has been good progress so far: increasingly
feasible to run sophisticated AI algorithms on ubiquitous edge-
devices.
VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
Deploying AI models on edge hardware offers benefits that
make systems responsive, private, and scalable. Their opti-
mization is therefore pertinent: the proposed approach tackles
the technological issues of joint packaging and inference at the
edge. In this research work, model optimization is proposed
under conditions that satisfy a selection of systems as a mutual
link between design principles and application potentials to
address MLOps and deep learning deployment dimensions.
Our experimental validation shows that our engineering can
deliver highly efficient inference on edge devices. The results
also show the value of the overall methodology: improvements
in speed, memory, and energy usage, with negligible impact on
accuracy. A correctly configured model, such as MobileNetV2,
reaches ∼100 ms on a Raspberry Pi machine limited by CPUs.
The same model without quantization was too slow for real-
time performance. This lowers the barrier for common ap-
plications—such as real-time image identification and sensor-
data-driven decision making—that were once discouraged on
edge devices for being too slow or power-hungry.
In the context of these experiments, a noteworthy observa-
tion is that a systemic approach yields maximum benefit from
quantitative model compression and packaging. Compression
is essential, and so is system-aware deployment. Although the
technique described in this article encourages model devel-
opers to pursue end-to-end deployment strategies that meet
latency and throughput specifications, additional flexibility for
development will be a future venture through integration.
Notwithstanding, the scaffolding laid out by this document
in terms of methodology and best practices will assist the
AI community and industry practitioners working on edge
AI applications. We aim to extend our methodology to more
model classes, like transformers for NLP at the edge, and
TinyML models for microcontrollers, as well as more use
cases for edge-based AI applications. The implementation of
automated optimization pipelines is on the roadmap. Dynamic
deployments, in this sense, are promising extensions for the
current study. In dynamic deployment, the system may decide
whether to run a model locally or in a cloud environment based
on contextual cues (such as available network conditions and
required accuracy). This stands as an extension of our static
deployment methodology. Such decision-making could enable
systems that are both efficient and flexible.
In an age where IoT- and mobile-centered artificial intelli-
gence are seeking optimal designs and applications, deploying
advanced AI on the edge—where data is generated—creates
new capabilities and a better user experience. This work
contributes to that vision by providing an understanding of
how to deploy complex algorithms under strict constraints.
By maintaining a view of mixed-model compression, hard-
ware acceleration, and deployment optimization, these findings
support practical, edge-first AI solutions that can be widely
distributed from cloud to edge.
